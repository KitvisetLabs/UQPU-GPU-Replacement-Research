# 03 — Fusion Electricity Cost-Minimization Strategy

**Permanent strategic pillar:** INV-032  
**Primary lanes:** E + D + G + F + H, integrated with all A–H lanes  
**Evidence status:** ACTIVE LONG-HORIZON RESEARCH / COMMERCIAL LOW-COST FUSION NOT_YET_DEMONSTRATED  
**Date:** 2026-09-10

# Highest-priority objective

Research how fusion energy could ultimately deliver **electricity as cheaply as physically and economically achievable**, because electricity cost propagates through essentially every future-industry stack in the Ketskaew Chulamani plan: computation, AI/data centers, semiconductor/quantum fabrication, materials refining, hydrogen, transport electrification, water, agriculture, robotics, cooling and future cities.

The target is not merely plasma ignition, fusion gain or a reactor physics milestone. The project-level target is **reliable delivered electricity at the lowest defensible lifecycle cost**.

## What must be measured

A fusion-electricity claim must eventually account for:

```text
LCOE-like plant cost =
  (annualized CAPEX
   + fixed O&M
   + variable O&M
   + fuel-cycle cost
   + component replacement
   + maintenance / remote handling
   + waste / decommissioning provisions
   + financing / insurance / compliance)
  / net electricity delivered
```

For system-level comparisons, grid/interconnect, firming/storage, transmission and required external infrastructure must be added when relevant.

The numerator must include the plant's own recirculating electrical load. The denominator is **net saleable electricity**, not gross fusion thermal power or gross generator output.

## Technical pathways kept in scope

Until evidence is stronger, research may compare:

- magnetic confinement, including tokamak and stellarator families;
- inertial-confinement / laser-driven approaches;
- magneto-inertial and pulsed fusion approaches;
- alternative fuel cycles and longer-horizon aneutronic concepts where scientifically justified;
- enabling technologies shared across approaches: superconducting magnets, lasers/pulsed power, blankets, heat extraction, tritium/fuel-cycle systems, neutron-resistant materials, robotics/remote maintenance, power conversion, diagnostics and manufacturing.

The project does not assume one architecture wins in advance.

## Cost-driving physics and engineering gates

Cheap fusion electricity requires simultaneous progress in more than fusion gain. Critical gates include:

1. sufficiently high **net electric gain** after recirculating power;
2. high availability/capacity factor and rapid maintainability;
3. long-lived plasma-facing components, blanket/divertor or chamber systems;
4. manageable neutron/material damage for neutron-producing fuel cycles;
5. credible tritium breeding, inventory and processing for D-T systems;
6. efficient heat-to-electric or direct-energy conversion where applicable;
7. low-cost magnets, lasers, pulsed-power systems and power electronics;
8. modular/standardized manufacturing and short construction schedules;
9. low replacement-part and remote-maintenance cost;
10. low financing cost and reduced first-of-a-kind risk.

A physics success that requires an excessively expensive, low-availability plant does not satisfy INV-032.

## Current public evidence boundary

The U.S. Department of Energy's finalized 2026 Fusion Science & Technology Roadmap is a serious commercialization roadmap and identifies science, technology, infrastructure and manufacturing milestones aimed at supporting fusion pilot plants/commercialization in the mid-2030s. It is **not evidence that fusion electricity is already commercial or low-cost**.

DOE itself states that significant scientific and technological challenges remain before fusion can reliably provide commercial electricity and other useful energy.

Therefore the repository treats present fusion-electricity prices as **unknown / not yet commercially measured** rather than assigning a speculative cheap number.

## Competitive price benchmark

Fusion should be compared against the best competing delivered-electricity options available at the time of each experiment, not against an outdated expensive baseline. IRENA's July 2026 global report gives useful dated reference points for newly commissioned generation, including global weighted-average LCOE around **USD 33/MWh for onshore wind and USD 44/MWh for solar PV in 2025**. These are generation-level benchmarks, not universal firm delivered-system prices, but they demonstrate that future fusion must compete with already-low-cost technologies.

The project's ambition is not merely to equal one dated benchmark. It is to continue minimizing total electricity cost while preserving reliability, safety, resource availability and manufacturability.

## Financing is part of the physics-to-economics bridge

Fusion is likely to be capital intensive. Longer construction time and higher perceived technology risk can substantially amplify electricity cost through financing. For this reason, reactor simplification, modular factories, component lifetime, construction speed and evidence-based derisking are economic design variables, not administrative afterthoughts.

INV-033 separately formalizes this financing/interest channel.

## Research architecture

### Lane D — device/plant physics
Translate candidate plasma/fusion architectures into measurable requirements for magnets, materials, heat loads, neutron environment, power conversion and lifetime.

### Lane G — manufacturing
Research low-cost high-throughput manufacturing of magnets, vacuum/target/chamber components, blanket modules, power electronics, lasers/pulsed systems, diagnostics and maintainable plant modules.

### Lane E — energy/materials/infrastructure
Own net-energy balance, thermal systems, materials, cooling, hydrogen integration and energy-system interfaces.

### Lane F — economics/evidence
Compute LCOE/system cost using measured/traceable inputs, sensitivity ranges and uncertainty. Never infer low cost from fusion gain alone.

### Lane H — financing/industrial strategy
Stage capital from simulation -> component tests -> subsystem prototypes -> pilot evidence -> commercial scale, minimizing capital exposed before uncertainty is reduced.

## Near-term research gates

1. Create a provider/technology-neutral fusion cost model based on net electric output, capacity factor, capex, component lifetime, replacement interval and financing.
2. Build separate evidence tables for tokamak, stellarator, inertial and pulsed/magneto-inertial routes.
3. Identify the top five contributors to hypothetical LCOE under wide parameter sweeps rather than assuming the reactor core dominates.
4. Model construction-time and WACC sensitivity explicitly.
5. Tie materials/manufacturing requirements to Lane D/G process-window contracts.
6. Track current public fusion roadmaps and experimentally measured milestones without promoting forecasts to demonstrated economics.
